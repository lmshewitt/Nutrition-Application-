Think like a food scientist

Nutrition values are classroom estimates from a small reference list, not laboratory measurements or medical advice. Actual values vary by brand, recipe, and preparation. Cups, teaspoons, tablespoons, and fluid ounces are converted using approximate ingredient-specific weights; ounces means weight, while fluid ounces means volume. For best accuracy, use grams or ounces by weight. Select cooked foods when measuring them cooked.

Nutrition Discovery

Nutrients are substances in food that help the body grow, repair, move, and function.

Macronutrients

Needed in relatively large amounts.

CarbohydratesProteinFat
  • Carbohydrates: the body's main energy source. Found in grains, fruit, milk, beans, and starchy vegetables.
  • Protein: helps build and repair muscles and other body tissues. Found in beans, eggs, dairy, fish, meat, nuts, and seeds.
  • Fat: provides energy, helps absorb vitamins A, D, E, and K, and supports cells. Found in oils, nuts, seeds, avocado, and dairy.

Micronutrients

Vitamins and minerals are needed in smaller amounts, but they are essential.

  • Vitamin C: supports healing and helps the body absorb iron.
  • Vitamin A: supports vision and immune function.
  • Calcium: supports strong bones and teeth.
  • Iron: helps red blood cells carry oxygen.
  • Potassium: helps nerves and muscles work.

Other important parts of food

  • Water: helps regulate body temperature and move nutrients.
  • Fiber: supports digestion and helps you feel satisfied. Found in plant foods such as whole grains, beans, fruit, and vegetables.

Explore the five food groups

Foods can provide nutrients from more than one group. These examples are a guide, not a rule that a food belongs to only one group.

Grains

Oats, rice, bread, pasta, tortillas.

Often provide: carbohydrates, fiber (especially whole grains), B vitamins, and minerals.

Try: choose whole-grain options often.

Vegetables

Broccoli, carrots, spinach, peppers, peas.

Often provide: fiber, vitamins A and C, folate, and potassium.

Try: include a variety of colors.

Fruits

Apples, bananas, berries, oranges, melon.

Often provide: carbohydrates, fiber, vitamin C, potassium, and other plant compounds.

Try: enjoy whole fruit often.

Protein foods

Beans, lentils, eggs, fish, poultry, nuts, seeds, tofu.

Often provide: protein, iron, zinc, and other nutrients. Nutrients vary by food.

Try: vary protein sources.

Dairy

Milk, yogurt, cheese, and fortified alternatives.

Often provide: calcium, protein, and (when fortified) vitamin D.

Try: check labels on fortified alternatives because nutrients differ.

Build a balanced plate

½ plate: fruits and vegetables

¼ plate: grains, with whole grains often

¼ plate: protein foods

Alongside: dairy or a fortified soy alternative, if included in the eating pattern.

This is a learning model inspired by the USDA MyPlate approach. Individual needs and cultural food traditions vary.

Cooking Method & Preparation Lab

Not every recipe uses heat. For a salad, “preparation” might mean washing, measuring, chopping, mixing, or tossing. For a baked dish, preparation happens first and baking is the cooking method.

Compare and reflect

Cooking can soften foods and make some nutrients easier to absorb. Heat and water can reduce some heat-sensitive or water-soluble vitamins. Frying can add fat and energy, depending on the food and amount absorbed. The effect varies by food and method.
